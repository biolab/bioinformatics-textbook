\chapter{Central Dogma of Molecular Biology}

The {\em central dogma of molecular biology} is a key idea that explains how genetic information is used in living organisms. Formulated by Francis Crick and published in 1958, it describes how genetic information is transferred between DNA, RNA, and protein, and---crucially---what transfers do {\em not} occur. This concept has been foundational in molecular biology and continues to shape our understanding of genetics.

Here are some of the major milestones, and some of the accompanying stories, in the development of the central dogma of molecular biology:

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1944:} Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty provided the first strong evidence that DNA is the genetic material. Studying pneumonia-causing bacteria, they found that traits such as virulence could only be transferred when DNA was present, while removing proteins or other molecules had no effect. At a time when most scientists assumed proteins carried genetic instructions because of their complexity, this experiment pointed instead to DNA as the true hereditary substance, a radical shift that set the stage for the discoveries that followed.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1953:} James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double-helix structure of DNA, building on X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins at King's College London. Franklin's X-ray diffraction work, including Photograph 51 produced with her PhD student Raymond Gosling, was critical to determining the structure, although her contribution was underrecognized at the time. Her X-ray diffraction data provided crucial constraints for constructing the correct molecular model.

James Watson and Francis Crick's famous paper, titled "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid," was published on April 25, 1953, in the journal Nature. This landmark paper was only about one page long, yet it had a profound impact on biology, as it proposed the first correct molecular model of DNA's double-helix structure.

Towards the end of Watson and Crick's 1953 paper, they included a famous line:
\begin{quote}
    \textit{It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.}
\end{quote}
This sentence is understated yet profound. In a single line, Watson and Crick hint at one of the most important implications of their discovery: the double-helix structure of DNA naturally explains how genetic information can be copied during cell division. The specific pairing between the nucleotide bases (adenine with thymine, and guanine with cytosine) suggested a mechanism by which each strand of the DNA molecule could serve as a template for creating a new, complementary strand. This subtle but powerful insight laid the foundation for understanding DNA replication, a central process that ensures genetic continuity from one generation to the next. The modest tone belied its transformative impact on genetics and molecular biology.

Notice that the discovery of the structure of DNA was challenging and marked by missteps, even by the most influential scientists of the time. Linus Pauling, 
\marginnote{Pauling won the 1954 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for revealing the nature of the chemical bond, especially the covalent bond. His book \textit{The Nature of the Chemical Bond} became a classic and guided generations of chemists and biologists---including those who later solved the structure of DNA.}
one of the most renowned chemists of the era, proposed a triple-helix model for DNA in 1953, with the phosphate backbone in the center and the nucleotide bases on the outside. This model was fundamentally flawed due to charge repulsion between phosphate groups and its failure to account for base pairing. Pauling's proposal, though influential, did not align with emerging data and was eventually replaced by the correct double-helix model proposed by Watson and Crick.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1955--1956:} Two enzyme discoveries opened new ways to study nucleic acids in the laboratory. In 1955, Severo Ochoa (with Marianne Grunberg-Manago) identified polynucleotide phosphorylase, which could assemble RNA-like polymers in a test tube without copying a template---a crucial tool for later genetic-code experiments. In 1956, Arthur Kornberg discovered DNA polymerase, the enzyme that synthesizes new DNA strands using an existing strand as a template. Together, these discoveries showed that the copying of DNA and RNA could be studied as a sequence of well-defined molecular operations carried out by enzymes. This was an important conceptual shift: the processes of life could be broken down into mechanisms that could be isolated, measured, and eventually reproduced in the laboratory.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1958:} Francis Crick introduced the term {\em central dogma} to describe allowed and forbidden routes of information transfer between DNA, RNA, and protein. His main claim was negative: once sequence information reaches protein, it cannot flow back to nucleic acids. The familiar pathway DNA $\to$ RNA $\to$ protein captures how genes are expressed, but Crick's original statement was broader and more cautious than the one-directional slogan often quoted today.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1961:} The existence of messenger RNA (mRNA) was demonstrated experimentally by Sydney Brenner, François Jacob, and Matthew Meselson, and independently by François Gros and colleagues in James Watson's laboratory. Jacob and Jacques Monod had proposed that an unstable RNA intermediate carries instructions from DNA to ribosomes; the 1961 experiments showed that new, short-lived RNA associates with existing ribosomes rather than requiring new ribosome synthesis---confirming mRNA's role as the link between genes and proteins.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1966:} The cracking of the genetic code was a major scientific breakthrough. A group of scientists, including Marshall Nirenberg and Har Gobind Khorana, worked tirelessly to decipher how RNA sequences are translated into amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. At the same time, the RNA Tie Club---an informal group of 20 scientists founded by George Gamow---added a mix of humor and camaraderie to the effort.

Each member was assigned one of the 20 amino acids, and the club even had specially designed ties and amino-acid tie pins. Although the club did not itself crack the genetic code, the correspondence and exchange of ideas among its members contributed to the intense theoretical discussion of how nucleotide sequences might specify proteins.

\bigskip\noindent\textbf{1970:} Howard Temin and David Baltimore independently discovered reverse transcriptase, an enzyme that copies RNA into DNA. The finding was initially described as a reversal of the central dogma, but Francis Crick noted that RNA $\to$ DNA had never been excluded from his original formulation. The enzyme provided crucial insight into how retroviruses such as HIV replicate by inserting a DNA copy of their RNA genome into the host cell.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1977:} Phillip Sharp and Richard Roberts independently discovered that some eukaryotic genes are not continuous but are interrupted by intervening sequences. These regions soon became known as introns, with the retained segments called exons. They showed that the corresponding RNA transcript is processed by splicing, which removes the intervening sequences and joins the remaining segments. This finding overturned the simple view of genes as uninterrupted stretches of DNA and revealed an additional layer of complexity in how genetic information is processed.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1977:} Frederick Sanger and colleagues introduced a practical method for determining the sequence of nucleotides in DNA.
%
\marginnote{In the same year, Sanger and colleagues determined the complete DNA sequence of a small virus, bacteriophage $\phi$X174---about 5,400 nucleotides. It was the first complete DNA genome to be sequenced. For comparison, one copy of the human genome contains about three billion base pairs.}
%
For the first time, researchers could read DNA as an ordered string of letters---A, C, G, and T---rather than only study it indirectly through experiments. Sanger sequencing became the dominant DNA sequencing technology for decades and eventually made large genome projects possible. For computer scientists, this was a crucial transition: genetic information became digital data that could be stored, searched, compared, and analyzed with algorithms.


\medskip\noindent\textbf{1980s:} The decade saw powerful new techniques that transformed molecular biology.
%
\marginnote{DNA sequencing quickly created a new kind of problem: where do we store all these sequences, and how do we search them? GenBank, a public database of DNA sequences, began in 1982 with only 606 sequences containing about 680,000 bases. The amount of sequence data has grown extraordinarily rapidly ever since, creating much of the need for bioinformatics.}
%
In 1983, Kary Mullis developed the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), allowing scientists to rapidly amplify DNA sequences in the lab. Soon after, Leroy Hood's team created the first automated DNA sequencing machines, opening the door to large-scale genomic projects. Together, these tools made it possible to copy, read, and analyze genetic material with unprecedented speed and accuracy.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{2000s:} The completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003 ushered in the genomics era. This international effort set out to determine a reference sequence of the human genome and identify its genes, producing a nearly complete sequence of the gene-rich portion of the genome. With this reference, researchers could study DNA, RNA, and proteins on a genome-wide scale, helping to accelerate fields such as comparative genomics, systems biology, and genomic medicine.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{2012:} Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna showed how the CRISPR-Cas9 system could be programmed to cut DNA at a chosen sequence. The key idea is remarkably simple: an RNA molecule acts as an address that guides the Cas9 protein to a matching DNA sequence, where Cas9 makes a cut. This turned a bacterial defense mechanism into a programmable tool for genome editing. Molecular biology had progressed from reading genetic information to modifying it at selected locations.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{2020:} A major computational breakthrough came with AlphaFold2, 
%
\marginnote{Only four years after AlphaFold2's 2020 breakthrough, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper received half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry ``for protein structure prediction.'' David Baker received the other half for computational protein design. The unusually short interval is striking: an advance based largely on algorithms, data, and machine learning transformed a major area of structural biology within just a few years.}
%
a deep-learning system developed by DeepMind for predicting the three-dimensional structure of proteins from their amino-acid sequences. Proteins begin as one-dimensional sequences of amino acids, but their biological function depends strongly on how these sequences fold into three-dimensional shapes. Predicting this structure had been a difficult problem for decades. AlphaFold2 showed that machine learning could predict many protein structures with striking accuracy. For computer scientists, this is an especially important milestone: one of the central problems of molecular biology became, to a large extent, a problem of statistical learning from biological data.

\bigskip\noindent
For computer scientists, the central dogma provides a blueprint for how biological data is organized. DNA is the database, RNA acts as a dynamic intermediary, and proteins are the functional executors. In bioinformatics, understanding these relationships is key to analyzing genetic sequences, predicting protein structures, and investigating the effects of mutations.
